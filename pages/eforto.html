Designing a clinically-validated, senior-friendly prototype for an EU-funded early health intervention pilot.

Tl;Dr

For an EU-funded pilot, I designed a Dutch-language prototype for a clinical app helping seniors track grip strength — an early indicator of health decline. The challenge: translate rigid medical test protocols into something a 75-year-old would confidently use alone. I led prototyping, ran guerrilla testing, trained non-designers to conduct usability sessions, and iterated based on feedback from actual geriatric patients.

The Story

When the brief comes with a clinical manual attached

The client's academic research team handed us a precise medical protocol: specific test sequences, required rest periods, exact timing constraints. These weren't suggestions — they were clinical requirements. My job was to make them invisible to the user.

I started by mapping the full protocol flow, then looked for parallels in interfaces seniors already trusted — fitness trackers and meditation apps. The insight: both are good at guiding users through multi-step processes with patience and clarity. We borrowed those patterns and adapted them to the medical context.

The constraint that shaped everything: the app had to work without any explanation from a clinician present. Seniors needed to complete the test independently, correctly, on the first try.

Building the prototype — and training a research team from scratch

I built a clickable Dutch-language prototype and immediately stress-tested it through guerrilla testing with friends and family — the fastest way to catch glaring issues before burning research budget on formal sessions.

The more unusual part: I then wrote a full usability test script and ran a roleplay-based workshop to train our non-design stakeholders to conduct sessions themselves. This wasn't just about scaling capacity — it gave them direct exposure to user behaviour, which changed how they talked about design decisions for the rest of the project.

Testing with the actual users — geriatric patients

Armed with the scripts and training, stakeholders facilitated usability tests directly with geriatric patients — both face-to-face and via video call. The feedback was specific and useful: instructions needed more reassurance, the progress indicator wasn't clear enough, and users needed to know what was coming next before committing to a step.

Each round of feedback led to concrete changes: clearer instructional copy, added microcopy that acknowledged the effort ("You're doing great — one more set"), and a visual progress indicator that showed exactly where the user was in the multi-step test sequence.
